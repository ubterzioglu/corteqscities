ESSENTIALS
Religion, art, culture and fervour
For more than seven centuries, Seville has been transformed every spring to intensely experience its Holy Week. Recognised as a Festival of International Tourist Interest, it is a unique experience that combines popular fervour, religiosity, history and art in the streets.
Its origins date back to the Middle Ages, although it was during the Baroque period that it acquired the artistic and symbolic richness that today defines this celebration, which is experienced intensely by the city and shared with those who visit it.
The dates of Holy Week vary each year, as they are determined by the lunar cycle and the spring equinox. Easter Sunday always falls between 22 March and 25 April.
In 2027, Holy Week in Seville will be celebrated from 21 to 28 March.
What happens during Holy Week?
For one week, Seville hosts daily processions that wind through its streets with different devotions representing the Passion, Death and Resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth.
Every day, several processions organised by their respective brotherhoods set out. These associations are grouped around devotion to an image of Christ or the Virgin Mary in order to worship them, receive spiritual training and carry out charitable works.
The processions depart from churches, basilicas and other temples scattered throughout different neighbourhoods of the city. All of them follow a common section of their route, known as the Carrera Oficial, which begins in the Plaza de la Campana and ends at Seville Cathedral. This visit to the Cathedral, called the Estación de Penitencia, is the central objective of each procession, after which they return to their temple of origin.
In total, dozens of brotherhoods and more than a hundred processional floats take part, allowing Holy Week to be experienced in different parts of the city and at different times of the day and night.

The city becomes an open-air museum
Holy Week in Seville is considered one of the world’s greatest outdoor artistic events.
The processional images are mostly Baroque sculptures from the 16th to 18th centuries, accompanied by embroidery, gold and silverwork, music, flowers and other handcrafted elements of great heritage value.
During these days, this heritage leaves the churches to tour the city and offer a unique spectacle to those who contemplate it.
Interesting facts about Holy Week in Seville
On Maundy Thursday and Good Friday, it is common to see many women dressed in mantillas, a tradition that forms part of the solemn and elegant character of these days. The black mantilla symbolises mourning and contemplation for the death of Jesus Christ and has its origins in ancient Spanish customs associated with both religious acts and solemn ceremonies.
Another unique experience is the Madrugá, which takes place from midnight on Maundy Thursday until the morning of Good Friday. During these hours, some of the most emblematic and fervent processions in the city take place.
The music varies according to the type of float. The floats of Christ are usually accompanied by bands of cornets and drums, while the floats of the Virgin Mary are accompanied by bands of music. There are also processions that proceed in complete silence, without musical accompaniment, belonging to more sober brotherhoods, popularly known as ‘serious’ brotherhoods in ‘black’.
As for the Nazarenes, there are two main types of robes: capes and tails. Tail robes, often made of black ruán, are characteristic of the more austere brotherhoods. In these brotherhoods, the Nazarenes remain silent throughout the procession and walk with their eyes fixed straight ahead. In contrast, in the brotherhoods with cape robes, the Nazarenes can interact with the public and it is customary for them to hand out wax, sweets or holy cards to children, giving the procession a more approachable and popular character.
Each brotherhood has its own identity. Colours, music, the way the float is carried and the style of the procession mean that no two processions are the same.
Holy Week is also experienced through the sense of smell. The aroma of incense, wax and orange blossom is an inseparable part of the experience.
You don’t have to be a believer to enjoy it. Many people experience Holy Week in Seville as a cultural, artistic and emotional experience, regardless of their beliefs.
The processions can last more than 10 hours. From the moment they leave their temple until they return, some brotherhoods spend almost half a day on the streets.
How to experience Holy Week
There are many ways to experience it: watching a procession, visiting churches, attending religious ceremonies and services, or simply letting yourself be swept up in the atmosphere in the streets, churches, and establishments.
If you wish to see the processions, we recommend checking their itineraries and schedules in advance in the Holy Week programmes produced by organisations and entities linked to the city, as well as in the digital and print media, which publish this information daily.
It is also advisable to arrive early at the busiest spots and combine the experience of the processions with traditional Lenten cuisine and visits to monuments and cultural sites, bearing in mind that many of them may change their opening hours during this period.

To bear in mind
01_
The costaleros: men carry the floats. They use a sash and a costal (a piece of canvas and burlap fabric around which a
kind of cushion is rolled up, fitting around the
neck to prevent rubbing from the working beam).
02_
Saetas are exciting flamenco songs dedicated to images, usually sung from balconies.
03_
The Nazarenes are brothers and sisters of a brotherhood who wear tunics and hoods to preserve their anonymity. They accompany the floats throughout the procession, carrying candles or other penitential symbols.
04_
The pasos are the structures on which the images are carried during Holy Week. There are different types of pasos, such as the mystery pasos, which represent scenes from the Passion, the pasos of Christ, and the characteristic palio pasos, under which the Virgin is carried.
05_
Procession marches constitute a musical genre in their own right, with works of great artistic value such as Amargura, La Madrugá and many others. These compositions accompany the processions and form an essential part of their atmosphere.
The marches are performed by bands of cornets and drums in the processions of Christ and Mystery, or by music bands in the processions of the canopy, adding rhythm, solemnity and emotion to the route.
Where to eat in Seville during Holy Week
La Semana Santa también se vive a través de la gastronomía. Entre procesiones, templos y recorridos, Sevilla ofrece barrios y rincones donde hacer una pausa y disfrutar de su cocina con más calma, sin alejarse demasiado del ambiente de estos días.
